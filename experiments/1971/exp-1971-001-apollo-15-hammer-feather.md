# EXP-1971-001: Apollo 15 Hammer and Feather Drop

## Hypothesis

According to Galileo Galilei's theory of falling bodies, in the absence of aerodynamic drag (air resistance), all objects will fall at the same rate of acceleration due to gravity, regardless of their mass. Therefore, dropping a heavy hammer and a light feather simultaneously in the vacuum of the lunar surface should result in them hitting the ground at the exact same time.

## Method

1.  **Setup**: The surface of the Moon (Apollo 15 mission, Hadley Rille). This environment provides a near-perfect vacuum and a consistent gravitational field (~1/6th of Earth's).
2.  **Materials**:
    -   A 1.32 kg (2.91 lb) aluminum geological hammer.
    -   A 0.03 kg (0.06 lb) falcon feather.
3.  **Procedure**: Commander David Scott holds both objects at the exact same height (approximately 1.2 meters) and releases them simultaneously.
4.  **Measurement**: Visual observation (and television broadcast) of the impact time of both objects on the lunar dust.

## Results

Both the hammer and the feather fell side-by-side, maintaining the same vertical position relative to each other throughout the descent. They impacted the lunar surface at precisely the same instant.

## Conclusions

The experiment brilliantly visually confirms Galileo's centuries-old hypothesis. In a vacuum, gravitational acceleration is independent of an object's mass. 

- This serves as a highly effective pedagogical demonstration of equivalence principles in physics.
- The mental model of gravity operating uniformly is visually validated for the public.
